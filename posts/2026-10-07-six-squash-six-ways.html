Every October the crate gets heavy, and most of the weight is squash. We grow six kinds in the field below the dinner table, and every Thursday somebody at the pickup holds one up and asks what on earth to do with it. So here is the cheat sheet I keep taped inside the field kitchen door, one squash at a time, in the order you will meet them.

Eat these first

Delicata. The striped one. Thin skin, so you eat it skin and all. Slice it into half-inch rings, scoop the seeds with a spoon, toss with oil and salt, and roast at 425 for 20 minutes, flipping once. Finish with hot honey. It keeps about two weeks on the counter, so it goes first.

Acorn. Dark green and ridged. Halve it, roast it cut side down until it gives when you press it, then fill the hollow with butter and maple, or with sausage and the last of the kale.

Spaghetti. The pale yellow football. Roast it whole, poked a few times with a knife, for an hour. Rake the inside with a fork and it falls into strands. Treat it like pasta, not like squash, and nobody at your table will complain.

These can wait

Kabocha. Squat and grey-green, and my favorite. It is dry and dense, almost like chestnut, and it wants a hot oven: 450, in wedges, skin on. The skin goes soft and you can eat it.

Butternut. You know this one. It keeps three months somewhere cool. Soup, risotto, or cubes roasted until the edges go black.

Blue Hubbard. The giant. It needs three more weeks on your counter before it is sweet, so do not cut it yet. When you do, use a cleaver, or drop it on the driveway inside a paper bag. We are serious. Roast the pieces, scoop the flesh, and you have pie filling for the whole winter.

The rule of thumb: the thinner the skin, the sooner you eat it. If your thumbnail goes in, cook it this week.

Storing them

Never the fridge. Squash likes it around 55 degrees and dry, so a closet floor or a basement shelf is perfect. Keep them apart so they do not touch, and check them on Sundays. A soft spot means that one goes in the oven tonight.

All six of these come to the table at the Harvest Supper on October 10, smoked over apple wood and served with cider pressed that afternoon. A few seats are left: write to the farm and we will hold one for you.

Dev Anand, chef, farm dinners
